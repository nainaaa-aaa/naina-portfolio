Compport · Enterprise SaaS · 2026

Org planning, without the twelve spreadsheets.

Compport is a financial HRMS platform for compensation and workforce budgets. Its enterprise clients planned restructures across disconnected spreadsheets and slide decks — no single source of truth, no audit trail, no shared view. I designed the org structure planning module from scratch; no legacy UI existed.

01 — Discover

Restructuring was happening in email attachments.

HR teams ran chart changes, role additions, budget reallocation and reporting-line shifts across dozens of spreadsheets shared over email. Nobody could say which version was current, draft, or approved — and nothing recorded who changed what, or why.

Half of HR leaders spend five or more hours a month just updating charts. A full cycle burned 40+ hours of reconciliation before anyone made a decision.

07 — Decisions

Four calls shaped the module. Each one traded a familiar, "safe" pattern for something that felt riskier to build — because the familiar pattern was the thing users associated with the problem in the first place.

Rejected

Tabular, spreadsheet-style data entry.

Shipped

A visual org chart with inline editing. Users equated spreadsheets with the problem itself — the interface had to feel fundamentally different to earn their trust. Tables are built for reading rows of data; they don't show relationships. A cell that reads "Manager: Sarah" doesn't tell you Engineering's backfill depends on Finance's sign-off — a linked org chart does, at a glance.

Rejected

A manual "save & send for review" handoff.

Shipped

A real-time workspace with role-based access. The email review cycle was Meera's single biggest time sink — four to five rounds per cycle, with no reliable way to tell which attachment was current. Making the workspace live meant Finance and Team Leads could react to a change the moment it was made, not three email threads later.

Rejected

Hidden dependencies, discovered at execution.

Shipped

A conflict panel, surfaced during planning. Cross-functional teams carried invisible assumptions about each other's moves — Engineering assuming a budget line Product had already claimed for a new hire. A visible panel, with the affected roles linked by a red dependency line, meant the clash got resolved on the canvas instead of blowing up mid-restructure.

Rejected

A standalone audit-log module.

Shipped

Contextual history on each node. Compliance teams need to know who approved a change, when, and under what conditions — usually under time pressure. A separate log forces someone out of their workflow to go find that answer; pinning the history to the node itself means the context travels with the change instead of living in a module nobody opens until an audit.

Fig 04 — the two decisions, side by side. Left: acting on a node without leaving the canvas. Right: the audit trail living on the node instead of in a separate module.

08 — The build

One canvas: structure, cost and consequence together.

Drag a person, open a vacancy, promote a lead — the modified cost figure updates against current cost in the same breath, and the legend explains every state change in plain language. Two weeks, four designers: I owned the chart canvas, node states and the compare flow.

The same structure also reads geographically. Clients running 600+ headcount per site kept asking a question the chart couldn't answer — where does this restructure land? Toggling to the geo view swaps hierarchy for footprint, from a world roll-up down to a single office and its department split.

10 — What I'd redo

Two weeks bought a strong canvas and a thin edge-case story. A 2,000-person chart with matrix reporting doesn't stay legible at the zoom levels I designed for, and I only found that out on the last day of the sprint.

Next time I'd prototype on the ugliest real dataset available before agreeing on the visual system, not after.

Meera didn't need a better spreadsheet. She needed to stop using one entirely.
