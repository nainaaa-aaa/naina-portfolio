What is this module?

Moving one person moves everything above them.

Compport is an enterprise compensation and HRMS platform. I designed its workforce planning module: the place where an HR team plans a restructure — promotions, transfers, vacancies — and sees what it costs before anyone signs off.

Before it existed, that planning happened in emailed spreadsheets.

We designed a single canvas to replace twelve emailed spreadsheets. Drag a person and the budget moves with them. Every change logs itself onto the node it happened to.

The analogy below is the fastest way to explain why the spreadsheets broke.

Picture a human pyramid. Everyone is standing on somebody's shoulders, and everyone knows exactly whose.

Now take one person out of the bottom row. Everything above them rebalances. Some of it holds. Some of it really doesn't.

Now do it with 2,000 people. On paper. From memory. While twelve other people are also quietly moving someone.

That's a restructure.

TL;DR

  • The problem. Planning a 2,000-person restructure ran on a dozen emailed spreadsheets. Nobody knew which file was current, and nobody could see the cost of a change until days after making it.
  • What I designed. A single org canvas where structure, cost and the audit trail live together. Drag a person and the budget moves with them.
  • The hard part. Four forks where the familiar pattern was the one users had learned to associate with the problem. We took the unfamiliar option every time.
  • The result. Planning one restructure was designed down from five or six weeks to under two, with every change documenting itself.

The problem

Enterprise HR teams ran restructures across a dozen spreadsheets emailed around the company. Nobody could say which file was current. Nothing recorded who moved whom, or why. Finance found out last.

What we built

A single live org canvas. Drag a role and the modified cost updates against current cost in the same breath. Conflicts surface while you plan instead of at execution, and the audit trail lives on the node itself, where the person asking the question is already looking.

And this is the part that stings: across a full cycle, this reconciliation ate more than 40 hours before anyone made a call. Half of HR leaders spend five or more hours a month just rebuilding charts. Teams lose about 3.6 hours a week to fixing spreadsheet mistakes, roughly 22 working days a year, per person.

02

Defining

One question to answer, and the two things it had to get right.

The challenge

How might we give Meera one place to plan a restructure, where Finance and her team leads see the same thing she does, and every change explains itself?

Project goals

Two things to get right.

Goal 1

Let people reach the org information they need without hunting for it across files, folders and inboxes.

Goal 2

Make the consequences of a change visible as it's made: cost, conflicts, and who signed off. That cuts anxiety and builds trust in the number.

Design decisions

Every time, the safe option was the thing users hated.

So we took the unfamiliar option four times. Here's each one.

Fork 01 · how you enter a change
✕ We said no to

Tabular, spreadsheet-style data entry.

✓ We shipped

A visual org chart with inline editing. Users equated spreadsheets with the problem, so the interface had to feel different. Unlike a row of cells, a linked chart shows the dependencies at a glance.

Fork 02 · how review happens
✕ We said no to

A manual "save & send for review" handoff.

✓ We shipped

A real-time workspace with role-based access. The email review cycle was Meera's biggest time sink; going live let Finance and team leads react the moment a change was made, not three threads later.

Fork 03 · when conflicts appear
✕ We said no to

Hidden dependencies, discovered at execution.

✓ We shipped

A conflict panel, surfaced during planning. Affected roles get joined by a red dependency line, so a clash gets settled on the canvas instead of blowing up mid-restructure.

Fork 04 · where history lives
✕ We said no to

A standalone audit-log module.

✓ We shipped

Contextual history on each node. Compliance can see who approved a change and when without leaving their workflow. The context travels with the change. Remember Meera's Friday? This is that fix.

Design

One canvas: structure, cost and consequence, together.

Solutions

Two weeks, four designers. I owned the chart canvas, the node states and the compare flow.

Drag a person, open a vacancy, promote a lead, and the modified cost figure updates against current cost in the same breath, and the legend explains every state change in plain language.

The structure reads geographically too. Clients running 600+ headcount per site kept asking the one thing the chart couldn't answer: where does this restructure actually land? Toggling to the geo view swaps hierarchy for footprint: a world roll-up, down to a single office and its department split.

06

What I took away

Two good weeks, and one lesson I'd rather have learned earlier.

Test on the ugliest data first

A 2,000-person chart with matrix reporting doesn't stay legible at the zoom levels I designed for — and I found that out on the last day of the sprint. Next time the ugliest real dataset comes before the visual system, not after.

The familiar pattern was the problem

Every fork here rejected the pattern people already knew. That felt risky each time. But the familiar pattern was the thing users had learned to associate with the problem, so shipping it would have been the bigger gamble.

Design for the people not in the room

Meera was the primary persona, but the module only works because Vikram and Priya can see it too. The best fix in the whole project (history on the node) was for someone who never touches the canvas.

Meera didn't need a better spreadsheet. She needed to stop using one entirely.
