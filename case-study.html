Compport · Enterprise SaaS · 2026

Org planning, without the twelve spreadsheets.

Compport is a financial HRMS platform for compensation and workforce budgets. Its enterprise clients planned restructures across disconnected spreadsheets and slide decks — no single source of truth, no audit trail, no shared view. I designed the org structure planning module from scratch; no legacy UI existed.

The short version

The problem
HR ran restructures across 12+ spreadsheets emailed round a company. Nobody knew which file was current, and nothing recorded who changed what.
What we tried
One visual canvas instead of cells: drag the org chart, watch the cost delta move live, and let every change log itself onto the node it happened to.
Where it landed
In moderated testing, 5–6 week planning cycles came down to under two, and 9 of 10 testers completed a restructure unaided.

07 · Decisions

Four calls, each one trading a safe pattern for a risky one.

Every time, the familiar pattern was the thing users associated with the problem — so shipping it would have been the actual risk.

Decision 01 · the interface model
✕ Rejected

Tabular, spreadsheet-style data entry.

✓ Shipped

A visual org chart with inline editing. Users equated spreadsheets with the problem itself — the interface had to feel fundamentally different to earn their trust. Tables are built for reading rows; they don't show relationships. A cell reading "Manager: Sarah" doesn't tell you Engineering's backfill depends on Finance's sign-off. A linked chart does, at a glance.

Decision 02 · the review loop
✕ Rejected

A manual "save & send for review" handoff.

✓ Shipped

A real-time workspace with role-based access. The email review cycle was Meera's single biggest time sink — four to five rounds per cycle, with no reliable way to tell which attachment was current. Making the workspace live meant Finance and Team Leads could react the moment a change was made, not three threads later.

Decision 03 · conflicting moves
✕ Rejected

Hidden dependencies, discovered at execution.

✓ Shipped

A conflict panel, surfaced during planning. Cross-functional teams carried invisible assumptions about each other's moves — Engineering assuming a budget line Product had already claimed. A visible panel, with affected roles linked by a red dependency line, meant the clash got resolved on the canvas instead of blowing up mid-restructure.

Decision 04 · where history lives
✕ Rejected

A standalone audit-log module.

✓ Shipped

Contextual history on each node. Compliance teams need to know who approved a change, when, and under what conditions — usually under time pressure. A separate log forces someone out of their workflow to find that answer; pinning history to the node means the context travels with the change.

08 · The build

One canvas: structure, cost and consequence together.

Two weeks, four designers. I owned the chart canvas, the node states and the compare flow.

Drag a person, open a vacancy, promote a lead — the modified cost figure updates against current cost in the same breath, and the legend explains every state change in plain language.

The same structure also reads geographically. Clients running 600+ headcount per site kept asking a question the chart couldn't answer — where does this restructure land? Toggling to the geo view swaps hierarchy for footprint, from a world roll-up down to a single office and its department split.

10 · What I'd redo

Two weeks bought a strong canvas and a thin edge-case story.

The part I got wrong is the part I found on the last day.

A 2,000-person chart with matrix reporting doesn't stay legible at the zoom levels I designed for, and I only discovered that at the end of the sprint.

Next time I'd prototype on the ugliest real dataset available before agreeing on the visual system, not after.

Meera didn't need a better spreadsheet. She needed to stop using one entirely.
