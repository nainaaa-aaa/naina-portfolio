What is Edme?

One system where there were several.

Edme is a motor insurance platform built for dealership networks. A dealer uses it to sell a policy, renew it a year later, and file a claim against it — work that previously meant moving between separate insurer portals with nothing joining them up.

Five roles share the platform, from the dealer at the counter to the insurer approving a discount, all on one reporting hierarchy.

In brief

  • The problem. A dealer selling motor insurance worked across disconnected insurer portals, manual data entry, and no visibility into leads, renewals, or claims once they left the counter.
  • What I designed. The core of the platform: a role-based dashboard, the quote-to-issuance sales funnel, the renewals flow, and the claims intimation flow.
  • The constraint. A complete functional specification, not an open research brief — roughly 200 screens delivered in 20 days, across two designers.
  • The result. A single, consistent system across four large flows, ready to measure once the product is live.

01

Context

Who the platform is for, and what the work looked like before it.

The problem

Nothing held the policy after the customer left the counter.

A dealer selling motor insurance previously worked across separate insurer portals, manual data entry, and no system of record for leads, renewals, or claims once a customer left the counter. Retention depended on individual memory rather than the platform. Visibility above the dealer level was reconstructed after the fact rather than tracked in real time.

Edme replaces this with one system, used by five distinct roles across a single reporting hierarchy.

Who is on it

Five roles, one set of screens.

The dealer
Sells, renews, and files

At the counter. Sells the policy, chases the renewal, and files the claim when the customer comes back. The platform's primary operator.

Regional manager
Aggregates a territory

Sees the same cards as a dealer, summed across every dealership reporting to them.

National manager
Aggregates the network

The same screens again, one level up. Promotion should not mean relearning the product.

Edme operations
Runs the platform

Handles what the automation cannot: exceptions, approvals, and the cases that stall.

The insurer
Prices and approves

Receives proposals, approves or rejects discount requests, and returns decisions into the dealer's funnel.

Each role sees the same underlying screens — the same dashboard, the same listings — filtered and aggregated differently depending on where they sit in the hierarchy. The design problem was building one set of components that could serve all five without becoming five separate products.

02

Principles

Four rules, none of them in the brief, applied to all four flows.

How the system held together

Four principles were applied consistently across all four flows. None appeared as stated requirements in the specification; each was established early and held throughout.

Applied in quote, renewals, claims
Autosave at every step

A dealer is interrupted constantly. Nothing should be lost because someone walked up to the counter mid-form.

Applied in KYC, claims
Conditional fields removed, not disabled

A field that does not apply is taken off the screen rather than greyed out.

Applied in dashboard, listings
One card and table grammar, reused

The same component reads correctly for a dealer and a national head.

Applied in trends, SLA
Colour and plain language together

Status is never carried by colour alone.

These principles made a consistent system possible across roughly 200 screens delivered by two designers in twenty days.

03

Design

Four flows, and the call I made at each of them.

Solutions

The dashboard every role shares, then the three journeys that run off it: selling a policy, renewing one, and claiming against one.

01 The shared screens

The screens all five roles share.

Landing, dashboard and the customer record. A dealer sees their own counter; a national head sees the network. The cards are the same cards.

The decision · one dashboard or five

A separate dashboard per role, each built for its own audience.

One dashboard, where each card aggregates differently depending on the viewer's position in the hierarchy.

Five dashboards would have meant five layouts to maintain, and a different screen to relearn each time a dealer is promoted. A shared card grammar — headline figure, trend indicator, target line — made the same component legible to a dealer and a national head alike.

02 Quote to issuance

The sales funnel, from a vehicle to a paid policy.

A stepped flow that takes a customer's vehicle details, prices them against every insurer at once, verifies the customer, and collects payment.

The decision · the IDV field

A plain numeric input for Insured Declared Value, with the premium recalculated on submit.

A bounded control with the minimum, recommended and maximum values shown, recalculating the premium as it changes.

IDV is the single largest lever on premium. Showing the range and the recommendation in the control itself lets a dealer see the trade-off immediately, rather than resubmitting a form to find out.

The decision · a rejected proposal

Returning a rejected proposal to the first step, requiring the dealer to start again.

Routing it back with the rejection reason attached, to the exact step that needs correction.

This is the point in the funnel where a dealer is most likely to abandon the sale. The cost of getting it wrong is a lost policy, not just a frustrated user.

03 Renewals

One entry point, whatever the policy came from.

A renewal may be sourced from Edme, rolled over from another insurer, or arrive with no retrievable record. A dealer rarely knows which before they start.

The decision · one field or three forms

Three separate entry forms, one per renewal source.

A single search field that detects a policy number or a chassis number as the dealer types, and proceeds regardless of whether a record is found.

Renewals are a low-attention, reminder-driven task. Asking a dealer to classify the renewal type before starting adds a decision they are not equipped to make at that point.

04 Claims

A long form, filled at the worst possible moment.

A guided claim intimation, filed at a point of stress for the customer and often interrupted at the dealership counter.

The decision · conditional fields

Showing every field for every claim, with irrelevant ones greyed out.

Removing fields entirely when they do not apply — driver details for a parked vehicle, a workshop code for a theft claim.

On a long form completed under stress, a visible but irrelevant field is an additional decision for the user to make. Removing it reduces what has to be read, not just what has to be filled in.

Outcome

What the design was built to achieve, from where the dealer is standing.

What it changes

Edme has not launched yet.

It is in final development. The table below describes what the design was built to achieve, not a measured result.

Before → designed for

Across the four flows

Manual entry across separate insurer portalsA single funnel, dashboard to issuance
Renewals tracked by individual memorySurfaced automatically, one entry point regardless of source
Visibility above the dealer reconstructed after the factReal-time, role-based, through the same dashboard
Phone calls to check a claim's statusTracked from intimation to settlement in one listing
Design targets, not measured results. Edme is pre-launch, so none of this has been observed in use yet.

Once live, the figures I intend to track are time from lead to issued policy, abandonment at the insurer-decision step, renewal conversion within the due window, and claim draft completion rate.

05

What I'd do differently

Three things, from a job that ran at twenty days for two hundred screens.

Test the two highest-stakes steps before build

The insurer-decision branch and the conditional KYC step carry the most logic and the highest risk of abandonment. These decisions were reasoned from the specification rather than observed in use.

Document the pattern library as it formed

The conventions that held the system together — autosave, conditional fields, the shared card grammar — were identified in hindsight. A living reference from the outset would have made onboarding a third designer, or auditing consistency, faster.

Review the specification in one pass, first

Several inconsistencies in the source document were resolved individually as each flow reached them. One upfront pass would have caught more of them before they became isolated decisions.

A specification tells you what to build. It does not tell you what to keep the same across two hundred screens.
