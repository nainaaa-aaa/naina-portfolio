What is Altkred?

Factoring is selling an invoice. It isn't a loan.

Altkred is a marketplace where Indian SMEs sell their unpaid invoices to banks and NBFCs, who bid against each other to buy them. The seller gets cash in about two days instead of waiting ninety. The financier gets a verified asset it can actually collect on.

That one distinction, bought rather than lent against, explains nearly every decision on this page, so it is worth thirty seconds.

1 The invoice exists

An SME delivers goods. The buyer accepts them and says: we'll pay ₹38 lakh in 90 days.

2 A financier buys it

They pay the SME ₹37.2 lakh today. The ₹80,000 gap is their fee.

3 The buyer pays them

On day 90 the buyer pays the full ₹38 lakh, to the financier now rather than the SME.

That's it. No loan, no collateral. The financier bought something rather than lending against it. Reverse factoring is the same trade started by the buyer: a big company lists invoices it has approved, and its suppliers take the cash early. The big company's credit is what makes the rate cheap.

TL;DR

  • The problem. An SME has done the work and can't get paid for ninety days. A financier has money and no way to verify the invoice or enforce collection. Both sit still.
  • What I designed. The whole platform, end to end: verification and onboarding, a bidding marketplace, funding, and settlement — including every way settlement fails.
  • The hard part. Four user types share the same screens and none of them is the primary one. Each became a rule the interface had to keep at once.
  • The result. Accepted invoice to cash in hand goes from 60–90 days to two, with the invoice itself as the security instead of the factory.

01

Discovery

Who is stuck, what is stopping them, and how big the gap is.

The problem

Two people who should be doing business, and can't.

One has done the work and can't get paid. The other has money and no safe way to lend it.

The seller

He's already spent money he hasn't been paid.

Rakesh makes auto parts near Pune. Sixty staff. About ₹2 crore of his money is always sitting in unpaid invoices.

He paid for steel in March. He paid wages in March and April. The invoice pays out in June.

To bridge it he can pledge his factory, or give his buyer a 3% discount. The bank has said no twice.

"I don't need a loan. I've done the work and they've accepted the goods. I need the money when I need it."

The financier

She'd fund it tomorrow, if she could see it.

Ananya lends to SMEs at an NBFC. She has ₹40 crore to put out this quarter.

But she can't check the goods arrived. She can't check the buyer accepted the invoice. And if he doesn't pay on day 90, she has no way to collect.

So she passes.

"I'm not avoiding this market. I'm avoiding an invoice I can't verify and a payment I can't enforce."

Primary research

Four people, and no main one.

I interviewed sellers, financiers and Altkred's own relationship managers, and spent two weeks trying to pick a primary user out of them. Every version was worse. All four have to work in the same screens.

Secondary research

The law moved before the market did.

I started with a competitor teardown, which told me what the category looks like. What actually shaped the product was regulation: the MSMED Act's 45-day payment rule, and the Factoring Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2021, which lifted the principal-business test that had limited factoring to seven NBFCs. The supply of financiers was about to grow; nothing existed to let them judge an invoice.

An invoice is only worth money to a stranger if it can prove itself. That's what I was designing for.

02

Defining

With no primary user, the brief had to hold four people at once.

The challenge

How do you make an invoice prove itself to a stranger?

An invoice is only worth money to someone who didn't watch the work happen if the platform can vouch for it: that the goods arrived, that the buyer accepted them, and that there is a way to collect on day 90.

Design principles

Each of the four got a rule.

The four don't just share a marketplace. They share the same screens. So the design couldn't favour one without breaking another. Each persona became a rule the whole interface had to keep at once.

The seller
Never show the machinery

The SME sees a number and a date. Risk scoring, insurance, bid mechanics: all real, all hidden. Simplicity is the feature.

The financier
Everything to judge it, on one screen

Buyer, amount, due date, proof of delivery, and how reliably that buyer has paid. Enough to price it without leaving the view.

The buyer
Exactly one new step

One payment permission, asked once, inside a flow they're already in. Ask for anything more and they simply don't do it.

The RM
A way out of every dead end

When the automation fails, a human always has a logged action to take, never a support ticket and a shrug.

The golden path

Four stages, in the order the money moves.

Those four rules only resolve in one order: the order the money actually moves. So that's how the product is built, and how the design section reads: get on, price it, move it, close it.

04

Design

Four stages of one invoice, and the call I made at each of them.

Solutions

Each stage below is a screen set, the decision that shaped it, and what I turned down to get there.

01 Getting on

Verified before the money is urgent.

Nobody does paperwork well when they're desperate for cash. So all of it happens first: company details, documents, bank setup. The marketplace stays visible but switched off until it's done.

The call · the payment key

Hide the payment key in settings and let financiers find it.

Grey out Place Bid on the home page until it's added, and say why right there. Without the key we can't take money from their account, so they could win an invoice they're unable to pay for.

02 Pricing it

Three financiers competing for one invoice, on one screen.

Each listed invoice shows the buyer, the amount, the due date, proof of delivery, and how reliably that buyer has paid in the past. That last one matters most. An invoice is only as good as the person who owes it.

Financiers bid against each other. So the seller picks from several offers instead of taking the only one.

The call · how a bid is priced

One "interest rate" box on the bid form.

Four separate choices instead: the rate and how long for, who pays the interest, the late penalty, and whether it's insured. A bid is a decision about who carries the risk, which is exactly what both sides argue about later.

03 Moving it

Money out in 48 hours, without a single email.

Four things happen once a bid is accepted. The buyer signs a payment permission. Interest is deducted. Insurance is paid. The funds land.

Every one of those is a place the money can stop.

The call · when to ask the buyer

Ask the buyer to set up the payment permission after the bid is won.

Ask inside the bid approval instead: one screen, one action. Chase a buyer for it afterwards and you never get it. And without it, there's no way to collect on day 90.

The funding screens are still in build. The decision above is the one that shaped them.

04 Closing it

Day 90 collects itself — and has somewhere to go when it doesn't.

On the due date the payment pulls automatically. Usually it works. The rest of the time is where this product is really decided.

The happy path is about a fifth of the screens. A dead end here is somebody's payroll, so nothing ends in "contact support".

The call · where a default goes

Put insurance claims in a separate admin module.

Keep them inside settlement. The worst outcome deserves the clearest screen, and the person handling it is already looking at the failed payment.

06

What I'd do differently

Three things, and the first one cost me six weeks.

Draw the failures first

I designed the happy path in week one, then spent six weeks on what happens when a payment bounces. Starting at the bounce would have made the happy path better. That's where a payments product earns trust.

Stop ranking the users

The persona problem in discovery cost me two weeks before I gave up on ranking them. The personas only became useful once I accepted that all four had to fit the same screens.

Read the law before the competitors

I started with a competitor teardown. It told me what the category looks like. What actually shaped the product was the MSMED Act's 45-day payment rule, and the Factoring Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2021, which lifted the principal-business test that had limited factoring to seven NBFCs.

Rakesh was asking someone to treat his invoice like the money it already was.
